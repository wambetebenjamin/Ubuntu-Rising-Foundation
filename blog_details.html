What eleven mobile clinics taught us about trust

  • Dr. Faith Chebet, Health Programme Lead
  • 18 September 2026
  • Community Health

When we costed our first mobile clinic in 2021, we budgeted carefully for drugs, fuel, a clinical officer and a cold chain. We did not budget for the thing that turned out to matter most, because it does not appear on any invoice: showing up on the same day of the month for two years without exception.

In the first six months, attendance at our Mwanza route averaged nineteen people a day. The villages had seen outreach programmes before. They arrive, they photograph, they leave. Nobody rearranges their planting day around a van that may not come back.

We kept going. By month fourteen the same route was seeing a hundred and forty people a day and the community health volunteers were being asked to arrive earlier. Nothing about our medicine had changed. What changed was that the van had come fourteen times.

We did not need to be impressive. We needed to be predictable. Predictability is the cheapest thing we have ever bought and the hardest to sustain.

Dr. Faith Chebet

Across the eleven routes we now run in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda, we saw 9,700 people last quarter. Immunisation coverage in the villages we serve monthly has gone from 51 per cent to 88 per cent. Antenatal attendance has roughly doubled. These are not remarkable clinical outcomes — they are what happens when an ordinary service becomes reliable.

What it costs

One clinic day, serving around 140 people, costs KES 68,000: fuel, drugs, the clinical officer’s day rate, consumables and the stipends of six community health volunteers. Training and equipping one of those volunteers costs KES 24,000 and they typically stay with us for four years.

The expensive part is the commitment. A route cannot be funded for six months and then reviewed. We will not open a new route unless we hold two years of funding for it, which is why our Mwanza expansion appeal is for three routes and not eight.

What we are still getting wrong

Referral follow-up remains weak. Of the patients we refer to district hospitals, we only confirm arrival for about 60 per cent. We are piloting a phone follow-up protocol in two routes this quarter and will publish the result either way.

If you want to fund a route, or you are a clinician who can give two days a month, we would like to hear from you.
